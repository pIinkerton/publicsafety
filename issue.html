The Issue: Why Policing Capacity Matters

An Overview of the Issue

The problem, put simply, is whether Dublin has enough Gardaí, in the right places, to keep the city safe and provide residents with a sense of security. While public safety and policing capacity are two closely intertwined areas, they are not exactly the same. Policing capacity represents the practical side, concerning whether An Garda Síochána has the personnel, time, equipment, information, and support required to respond to and investigate incidents effectively. Public safety, by contrast, is what residents actually experience: whether they can live, work, study, and travel without unreasonable fear of crime or harm. Although closely connected, the two don't always align; concerns regarding safety can persist even when official policing statistics appear more positive.

What the Evidence Shows

Statistics suggest that the current situation is neither black nor white. Nationally, the government reports record high numbers of Gardaí, with 14,825 in total as of October 16 2026, compared to 14,491 in December 2020 and 14,377 in December 2010. However, it must be noted that Dublin has seen a reduction in the number of Gardaí serving its divisions, with only 3,868 in August 2026 compared to 3,944 in December 2020 and 4,225 in July 2010. The Assistant Garda Commissioner for Dublin has commented that the force is "down on numbers we would want to have". In response to concerns over Dublin's lack of Garda presence, the Dublin City Taskforce recommended deploying 1,000 additional Gardaí in Dublin city centre over three years in its October 2024 report, yet it remains unconfirmed as to when these 1,000 extra Gardaí will be deployed.

In 2010, 4,225 of the country's 14,377 gardaí served Dublin divisions, about 29% of the force.

By 2020, national strength had risen by 114, but Dublin divisions had lost 281 Gardaí.

Nationally, strength is currently 448 higher than in 2010, while Dublin divisions have 357 fewer. Dublin's now accounts for about 26% of Gardaí.

Who It Affects, and Why It's Urgent

This issue of policing capacity, and in turn public safety, impacts anyone who lives in, works in, or visits Dublin, including commuters, students, women, the elderly, shopkeepers, and suburban communities. The issue is urgent for a variety of reasons:

  • Fear can act as an impediment to culture. When people avoid certain streets, evenings out, or public transport, Dublin loses some of the vibrancy and cultural life I previously described as part of what makes the city so special.
  • Public trust in An Gardaí Síochána may decline. Gardaí depend on the public to report criminal activity. However, when people feel their concerns are going unheard, some may lose confidence in the system and stop reporting incidents altogether. This creates a further problem: if fewer crimes are reported, recorded crime statistics may be artificially deflated while not necessarily indicating improved public safety.
  • Dublin's policing capacity could come under further pressure. Even if the government committed tomorrow to putting more Gardaí on Dublin's streets, training new recruits takes time. Paired alongside a predicted wave of retirements due to accelerated Garda recruitment 30 to 40 years ago, unless current recruitment keeps pace with these departures, policing capacity in Dublin could fall further.

Having lived in Dublin all my life, I have already begun to see these fears become reality. My mother often asks that I head up to our local SuperValu in the evenings, feeling too uneasy to walk there herself after dark. Friends have suggested taking different routes to avoid that one street they've heard bad stories about. I have even found myself sometimes avoiding late nights in town, opting instead for an earlier, less chaotic bus home. None of these examples is particularly stark on its own, but together they capture the creeping sense of discomfort and fear that increasingly comes with being a Dublin resident.
